Three directions for the same five screens
Each row is one direction, drawn across the identical slice: the menu-bar surface, the visual-reset heads-up, the visual-reset overlay, the movement overlay, and the schedule settings pane. Reading down a column compares how the three directions solve the same problem.
The axis they differ on
How much of the screen a break is entitled to — which is the product's central question, not a stylistic one.
A Margin
No container at all. The desktop dims and one line of type sits in the space. The app is a margin note, not a screen.
Bet: nothing to tire of on the 500th viewing.
Cost: four equal movement options and a mandated safety line are hard to hold without structure.
B Field
A wide, calm band across the middle of the dimmed screen. Recognisable by its shape from the corner of the eye, before any reading happens.
Bet: half-second recognition without reading.
Cost: the heaviest treatment of the three, and the closest to what the category already does.
C Instrument
A compact, precise panel in the corner over a barely-dimmed desktop. A break is an offer from a well-made tool, not a takeover.
Bet: the most obviously native, the least intrusive.
Cost: easy to ignore entirely, and density under interruption raises cognitive load.
What is fixed in all three
Escape exits every surface, no confirmation
The overlay dims the desktop; it never replaces it
Movement options carry equal weight, not a fallback
Menu-bar glyph is monochrome, no badge, no countdown
Nothing is verified — completion is a user statement
Nothing escalates on the fifth decline
Copy is drawn from the approved register, verbatim
No score, no streak, no posture, no blue light
